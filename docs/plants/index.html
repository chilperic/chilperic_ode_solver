How the three plant models work

C3 · direct fixation

Light supplies ATP and NADPH. CO₂ passes through stomata and is fixed through a Rubisco/light-limited calculation, with photorespiratory effects. The resulting carbon supports reserves, respiration and tissue construction.

Best question: how do CO₂, temperature, water restriction and light change carbon gain?

Missing: mesophyll diffusion, dynamic stomatal regulation, canopy shading and species fitting.

C4 · spatial concentration

PEPC pumping, sheath leakage and biochemical fixation are coupled to stomatal and mesophyll diffusion. Bundle-sheath CO₂ and leakiness emerge from that balance. Pumping incurs 2 ATP per realized turnover; the energy budget partitions ATP between pumping and fixation.

Best question: how do pump capacity, leakage and energy supply interact in this approximation?

Missing: separate cell energy/redox transport, variable sheath O₂, subtype-specific decarboxylation and fitted species parameters.

CAM · temporal concentration

Nighttime atmospheric uptake fills a finite carbon store and incurs a reserve-funded energy cost. Daylight releases stored carbon, limited by biochemical and light-energy capacity. Release is not counted twice.

Best question: how do storage, night uptake and day/night conditions change water use and carbon gain?

Missing: phases II/IV, circadian regulation, malate/PEP stoichiometry and facultative CAM switching.

Mechanism, scales and assumptions

This is your C4-only cooling hypothesis. The controller first solves leaf temperature with transpiration active and circulation absent. If that temperature exceeds the selected target, it uses up to the permitted return flow, provided the soil can absorb heat. The right plant can therefore show standby, active regulation, or an unmet target. OFF always has zero circular return.

The animation contains no flowing-water paths or transport particles. Soil indicators use the actual water and available-nitrogen pools; plant uptake, evaporation and leaching reduce them, while rainfall, irrigation and fertilizer can replenish them. These are availability gauges, not a spatial water-table model. Water is scaled by soil capacity; nitrogen by initial available nitrogen (1000 mg if initially zero), capped visually at a full gauge with the actual amount printed. Drought resilience & target search

Compare the last completed run with drought and drought + heat. Drought removes rain and irrigation during the chosen window. Heat raises temperature at approximately constant air vapour pressure, increasing VPD. A recovery pulse restores water; other original conditions resume. This is a controlled stress test, not a climate-change projection.

Production matters: every feasible candidate must meet both absolute harvest-tissue thresholds and the selected recovery threshold. Harvest tissue is a dry-mass proxy, not predicted edible yield, viable seed number or fitness. Choose the organ appropriate to your crop and measured yield targets; the displayed defaults are illustrative. Grain filling, harvest index and viable seed production require further model development.

Productivity requirements prevent low growth from being rewarded simply for conserving water. Recovery is carbon uptake during the third day after drought, relative to the control over the same hours. Tissue loss is the sum of hourly decreases in structural dry mass; the model has no death or survival probability.

Where did the assimilated carbon go?

Retained carbon change = atmospheric uptake − maintenance respiration − growth respiration − CAM night-energy respiration − litter carbon. CAM release transfers existing carbon internally; it is not counted as a second atmospheric uptake.

Carbon concentration & water-use efficiency

C3 fixes carbon after diffusion into the leaf. C4 solves mesophyll and bundle-sheath CO₂ from PEPC pumping, Rubisco fixation, sheath leakage and diffusion. In energy-budget mode, pumping and fixation share the available ATP and NADPH. CAM takes up CO₂ at night and releases stored carbon by day. These mechanisms remain uncalibrated for the illustrated species.

Uptake excludes whole-plant maintenance; these are uptake-based efficiencies, not measured net gas-exchange WUE. Intrinsic WUE = uptake / stomatal conductance (µmol CO₂/mol H₂O conductance). Cumulative carbon WUE = acquired g C / litres transpired. Growth WUE = gross new dry tissue / litres transpired; it includes reserve-supported growth, so it is not net biomass gain. Instantaneous WUE = uptake / transpiration. Carbon per received photon is a cumulative operating ratio, not an absorbed-light quantum yield. Ratios show “—” before meaningful water use. C4 pump and leakage are balanced realized rates; a negative leakage value means inward diffusion into the sheath. CAM daytime internal CO₂ remains prescribed, not solved.
What does it take to build leaves, stems, flowers or fruit?

Carbon substrate includes growth respiration. Water transpired and photons received are measured over this simulation; they are not fixed ingredients per leaf. Tissue hydration is not modeled. Initial organs were supplied at the start and are excluded from new construction.

Anatomy, development & transport: evidence and hypotheses

Vein density: shorter post-vein water pathways can support hydraulic and photosynthetic capacity, but leaf thickness, cell geometry and conduit properties also matter. More veins are not a universal route to higher yield. Brodribb, Feild & Jordan (2007): comparative measurements across 43 species.

Root density: root length density differs from root mass, root hair abundance and rooting depth. Water access also depends on soil hydraulic properties, root–soil contact and drying. Duddek et al. (2023): maize microtomography and image-based uptake modeling. This app reports equivalent root density but still has one shared water bucket.

Kranz anatomy: typical C4 maize combines closely spaced veins, specialized mesophyll and bundle-sheath cells, compartmentalized enzymes and controlled CO₂ leakage. Vein density alone does not create C4 photosynthesis. Billakurthi et al. (2024): developing C3/C4 Flaveria leaves. Kranz is not universal: Single-cell C4 transcriptomics study (2023), Bienertia demonstrates a distinct compartmentation strategy. The current C4 model solves a reduced mesophyll–sheath carbon balance; it does not generate Kranz anatomy from vein spacing.

Development: sowing → emergence → vegetative leaf appearance → flowering → reproductive filling → maturity is a useful crop sequence, but species, cultivar, temperature, photoperiod and vernalization alter timing and overlap. C3, C4 and CAM are photosynthetic pathways, not life-cycle categories. Maize does not initiate main-stem leaves indefinitely. Dos Santos et al. (2022) found a two-phase phyllochron fit more accurate than a constant one; Plancade et al. (2023) modeled successive leaf appearances as time-to-events. Our constant phyllochron and editable thresholds are an exploratory starting point, not those fitted models.

Translocation: photosynthetic sources supply growing sinks; reserves and senescing tissues can contribute carbon or nutrients during reproduction. Knoblauch et al. (2016) experimentally support pressure-driven phloem transport. Our allocation arrows represent carbon accounting, not a solved vascular pressure network. Stem-to-reserve mobilization debits the donor carbon and recovers its nitrogen before new tissue can be built.

Your C4 cooling hypothesis: transpiration exports latent heat; an additional circulation loop could redistribute sensible heat. Circulation cooling is conditional on a cooler receiving compartment. Xylem–phloem transport does not by itself establish a radiator-sized heat flux. Compare the hypothesis against the loop-off model and measured temperatures, transpiration, sap flow and soil heat. Default loop flow and effectiveness are assumptions, not literature fits.

Calibration priorities

Use leaf images for vein length / area; root scans and soil cores for specific root length and depth-dependent density; tagged-leaf time series for phyllochron and final leaf number; flowering and filling dates for phenology; gas exchange, thermography, sap flow and soil thermistors for heat/water; organ dry mass, nitrogen and tracer pulse–chase measurements for allocation. Fit physiology and anatomy jointly, retain independent validation dates, and compare yield under drought at a minimum acceptable unstressed yield.

Temperature, water and evaporation

These equations are visible without running an experiment. Change humidity, water conductance or soil temperature, then inspect the temperature, VPD, transpiration and evaporation plots.

1. Air humidity and leaf-to-air vapour pressure deficit

Dleaf=max(0,esat(Tleaf)−RHesat(Tair))

Dleaf = max(0, esat(Tleaf) − RH × esat(Tair)). RH is a fraction, so 65% is 0.65. Air humidity refers to air temperature, while saturated vapour pressure inside the leaf refers to leaf temperature. Dew is excluded by the maximum with zero.

2. Saturated vapour pressure

esat(T)=610.8exp(17.27TT+237.3)

esat(T) = 610.8 exp[17.27 T/(T + 237.3)] Pa, with T in °C. The heat solver uses kelvin internally and converts for this approximation.

3. Stomata and boundary layer act in series

gtw=11gsw+1gbw

gtw = (1/gsw + 1/gbw)⁻¹. gsw is stomatal conductance to water; gbw is boundary-layer conductance to water. Units: mol m⁻² leaf s⁻¹. gm in the carbon model is mesophyll CO₂ conductance and is not substituted into this evaporation equation.

4. Leaf transpiration and evaporative cooling

E=gtwDleafP

E = gtw Dleaf/P, mol H₂O m⁻² leaf s⁻¹. D and atmospheric pressure P must use the same units. Latent heat loss is λE; growth uses λ = 44,000 J/mol and P = 101,325 Pa. The research adapter exposes its pressure and uses its source λ. At fixed temperature/conductance, higher RH reduces E; the coupled leaf temperature can change in response.

5. Exposed soil evaporation

Esoil=gsoilw2max(0,esat(Tsurface)−RHesat(Tair))P

Esoil = gsoil w² max[0, esat(Tsurface) − RH esat(Tair)]/P, mol m⁻² ground s⁻¹. w is relative bucket wetness. This conductance law is illustrative, not a fitted soil evaporation model. Growth limits E and Esoil jointly by available water; transpiration has priority.

6. Solved leaf temperature

0=Qabsorbed−Hair−λE−Rsky−Rsoil−Qxylem−Qloop

Absorbed shortwave − convection − latent cooling − net longwave to sky and soil − sensible heat gained by xylem water − optional circulation heat = 0, W m⁻² leaf. H = h(Tleaf − Tair); growth uses h = 20 + 15√wind W m⁻² K⁻¹. Temperature is a solved state, not an imposed offset. Leaves are quasi-steady; no leaf heat capacity is included.

7. Soil surface heat storage

CsurfacedTsurfacedt=Qsoil+LAIRleaf,soil−Rsoil,sky−Hsoil−λEsoil−Gsurface,root−Qwater,surface

Surface heat storage = absorbed shortwave + leaf longwave input − longwave to sky − convection − evaporation − conduction to roots − water-redistribution sensible heat. Soil fluxes use W m⁻² ground. Soil convective conductance is 8 + 4√wind in growth and 12 W m⁻² K⁻¹ in the research adapter.

8. Root-zone heat storage

CrootdTrootdt=Gsurface,root−Groot,deep+Qwater,root+LAIQloop

Root-zone storage = conductive input from surface − conduction to deep soil + sensible heat from water redistribution + LAI × optional circulation heat. Gsurface,root = k(Tsurface − Troot)/[(dsurface + droot)/2]. Groot,deep = k(Troot − Tdeep)/(droot/2 + 0.5 m). The deep temperature is a fixed boundary, not another solved layer.

9. Heat carried by transpiration water

Qxylem=75.2E(Tleaf / sheath−Troot)

Qxylem = 75.2 E(Tleaf/sheath − Troot). Water heat capacity is approximated as 75.2 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹. Positive cools the leaf; negative warms it. The flow equals actual transpiration. This once-through flux remains distinct from the additional closed circulation hypothesis. It is not returned to the root zone.

10. Soil water balance

ΔWsoil=Wrain+Wirrigation−Woverflow−Wtranspiration−Wevaporation

W is mm over the fixed 0.25 m² ground footprint. For Δt hours: transpiration removes 64.8 E × LAI × Δt mm, and soil evaporation removes 64.8 Esoil × Δt mm. The latent cooling and water loss use the same flux, debited once. The research steady-state adapter instead prescribes wetness and checks E ≤ Kl(ψsoil − ψleaf,limit).

11. Growth-module stomatal response

gsw=g0+gmaxopenswater1+Dleaf,kPa2

Before hydraulic restriction: gsw = (0.002 + gmax × open × swater)/(1 + Dleaf,kPa/2). gmax is 0.22 for C3/C4 and 0.16 for CAM; these are illustrative constants. swater = clamp[water/(0.4 capacity), 0, 1]. C3/C4 opening = mean leaf PAR/(mean leaf PAR + 150); CAM opens at night. Water supply can reduce gsw further. The research adapter instead lets you enter or optimize gsw directly.

12. Root water-supply constraint

Wallowed=min(Wrequested,Wroot supply,Wavailable)

Growth limits requested transpiration by 0.05 × root dry mass × relative wetness L/h, and by the water available over the step. The coefficient is illustrative. Conductance is reduced to deliver that allowed flux while retaining the boundary-layer resistance. Soil evaporation then uses remaining water. These constraints are distinct from the research adapter’s water-potential inequality.

13. Optional closed circulation radiator

Qloop=I75.2Floopε(Tleaf / sheath−Troot)

I is zero for C3/CAM and for C4 when disabled or when its no-circulation leaf temperature meets the target. For eligible C4, Floop is selected by a bounded search from zero to the user maximum, after a transpiration-only heat solve. The flow stops if soil cannot receive heat. Floop is actual circulating water in mol m⁻² leaf s⁻¹; ε is an effective heat-exchange fraction. Qloop is W m⁻² leaf. The leaf loses Qloop and root soil gains LAI × Qloop, so internal transfer cancels in the whole-system heat balance. Circulation consumes no water; it may indirectly alter transpiration by changing temperature. Warmer root soil disables supplementary circulation. The maximum flow and temperature target are hypothesis parameters, not measured phloem regulation. The closed path is soil exchange → roots → xylem stem → leaf → phloem stem → roots → soil exchange; upward flow equals transpiration plus circulation, downward flow equals circulation. Thus net soil loss equals transpiration. Vascular pressures, transit times and internal plant water storage are unresolved. No pump-energy or anatomical feasibility constraint is included. Prescribed soil temperatures imply an external thermal bath.

14. Root length density

RLDcm/cm³=0.0001Mroot,drySRLm/gAgroundzrooted

RLD = 10⁻⁴ × root dry mass × specific root length / (ground area × rooted depth), in cm root per cm³ soil. Vein length density is mm vein / mm² leaf. Total vein length in metres = 1000 × density × leaf area in m². These are geometric descriptors, not universal hydraulic conductances.

15. Development clock and phyllochron

Δτthermal=max(0,min(Tdriver,Tceiling)−Tbase)Δthours24

Thermal time is °C days. Temperature driver is root-zone temperature before emergence and air temperature afterward. Wetness scales the pre-emergence increment. Divide accumulated time by the selected pathway multiplier for stage and leaf thresholds. Emergence, flowering, filling and maturity thresholds are editable; appearance is gated by a constant phyllochron, final leaf ceiling and flowering. Resource availability controls actual expansion. This does not reproduce photoperiod or vernalization responses.

16. Conservative stem mobilization

ΔCmobilized=Cstem(1−exp(−kmobilizeΔthours24))

During thermal-time filling, transfer ΔC from stem to reserve; recover ΔC / carbon fraction × tissue nitrogen fraction to the N reserve. Total C and N are unchanged by transfer. Later construction pays growth respiration and draws N. Leaf construction beyond available appearance capacity is redirected to stem. This is a coarse turnover hypothesis, not an explicit stem nonstructural-carbohydrate or phloem transport model.

Closures, units and numerical solution

Longwave leaf-to-soil transfer is εpair σ F(Tleaf⁴ − Tsurface⁴), where εpair = (1/εleaf + 1/εsoil − 1)⁻¹, soil emissivity is 0.96, and all radiative temperatures are kelvin. F = min(user fraction, 1/LAI) enforces reciprocal area exchange; soil receives LAI times the leaf-area flux. Leaf sky fraction is 1 − F; soil sky fraction is 1 − LAI F. Effective sky temperature = air temperature − the editable sky offset. This is aggregate exchange geometry, not resolved canopy radiation.

Ci = [cdry + 4.18 × 10⁶ θ] di J m⁻² K⁻¹, where θ = bucket water (m)/total modeled soil thickness. k = kdry + (kwet − kdry)w. Dry heat capacity and this linear conductivity interpolation are assumptions. The bucket tracks available water only; residual bound water, freezing, snow and dew are not represented. Subzero results are an unfrozen extrapolation.

The uniform-moisture bucket redistributes water between layers. With fs = dsurface/(dsurface + droot), Qwater,surface = 75.2 Esoil(1 − fs)(Tsurface − Troot), and Qwater,root = 75.2 LAI E fs(Tsurface − Troot). Accepted rain/irrigation mixes at air temperature; overflow bypasses mixing. Water temperatures and infiltration fronts are not resolved.

Growth uses backward Euler at 10-minute steps for soil storage and a coupled nonlinear solve. Canopy interception = 1 − exp(−0.6 LAI); mean received PAR = intercepted PAR/LAI, absorbed leaf shortwave = 0.65 × intercepted solar/LAI. These optical coefficients are illustrative. Leaf temperature drives photosynthesis, shoot maintenance and growth mobilization; root temperature drives root maintenance. Hydraulic conductance and N uptake have no additional calibrated root-temperature response.

The research model retains its two optical compartments and source optical coefficients, divides effective external longwave area equally, and adds conduction UA(Tmes − Tbs). Mesophyll loses latent heat; the sheath exchanges sensible heat with incoming xylem water. Its four temperatures are either solved at equilibrium, or surface/root temperatures are held by external baths. Its Jmax remains temperature-independent. Chemical energy stored in biomass and organ heat storage are omitted in both thermal approximations.

Evidence and calibration

Your C4 draft, section 17 (PDF pp. 61–64), motivates the separate soil temperatures and heat stores. Assumptions, remaining model rules & verification

This is an exploratory implementation. It does not reproduce Photo3 or the full von Caemmerer 2021 model, and it has not been fitted to species measurements. Your existing Python research model is not connected here.

C3 & C4

C3 uses Rubisco/light limitation with photorespiration and a diffusion solve. C4 now solves A = Vp − L, L = gbs(Cbs − Cm), and the stomatal/mesophyll diffusion balances. Potential PEPC activity, PEP regeneration and the pump ATP allocation limit Vp; Rubisco, NADPH and the remaining ATP limit fixation. Kc, Ko, PEPC kinetics and mesophyll conductance use C4 reference values and temperature responses from von Caemmerer (2021). Vcmax/Jmax temperature curves and species capacity scaling remain illustrative. Oxygen is fixed at its atmospheric value; leaf respiration is charged only in the whole-plant ledger. The current concentration model is a reduced implementation, not the full cell-resolved model. Legacy mode retains the prior empirical light limitation but still solves carbon and diffusion balances.

CAM

A prescribed light/dark switch controls nocturnal uptake into a finite store of newly fixed carbon. Daylight releases this store into reserves, limited by light-dependent assimilation capacity and a four-hour release time. Carbon above the remaining tissue storage capacity after tissue loss is exported to litter. There is no endogenous circadian oscillator, CAM phase II/IV uptake, respiratory refixation, or explicit malate/PEP stoichiometry in this version.

Carbon & growth

Total plant carbon = leaf + stem + root + flower + fruit carbon + reserves + CAM store. In elapsed-day mode, after a selectable day the reproductive allocation draws from the same construction budget. Each cohort builds a 0.025 g dry flower before filling its fruit. Flower and fruit tissue use the same carbon fraction, construction yield and nitrogen fraction as other tissues. Their illustrative maintenance rates are 1.5% and 1% of tissue carbon per day at 25 °C; turnover is 3% and 0.1% per day, exported to litter. Optional thermal-time development is available. Pollination failure, harvest and species-specific viable-seed models are not implemented. Photosynthetic input adds carbon; maintenance respiration, growth respiration and litter remove it. Growth mobilizes reserves above a 2% structural-carbon buffer with a two-day time constant, modified by temperature and soil-water stress. Carbon yield is editable (default 75%); fixed organ allocation and available nitrogen constrain construction. There is no pathway-specific biomass multiplier. Similar growth can still occur under a common nitrogen constraint; compare carbon uptake and reserves as well as biomass. Nitrogen recovery from senescing leaves and stems is a coarse resorption model, shared across pathways and not calibrated by species. It supports subsequent construction from the common reserve without adding carbon or nitrogen. It does not represent active protein degradation, phloem transport or organ-specific nitrogen concentrations. Large carbon reserves with stalled tissue growth indicate a nutrient or sink constraint, not productive yield. Maintenance uses reserves first, then structural carbon if necessary.

The uptake chart includes photorespiratory effects but precedes whole-plant maintenance costs; it is not net leaf gas exchange. CAM daytime release is internal transfer and is never counted twice as new carbon.

Water & temperature

A plant-available water bucket tracks rain, irrigation, overflow, soil evaporation and transpiration. The soil-N pool receives fertilizer and loses N through plant uptake and well-mixed overflow. After reproduction is enabled, a selectable fraction of N from senescing leaves and stems is recovered into the plant reserve before litter export. Roots, flowers and fruit do not contribute to this recovery rule. The remaining N leaves with litter; this is not soil mineralization. Nitrogen limits new structural growth. There is no mineralization, microbial competition, fixation, or direct leaf-N regulation of Vcmax. Root mass and remaining water limit uptake. A simplified steady leaf-energy balance links radiation, wind and evaporative cooling. Explicit water-potential dynamics, leaf heat storage, phosphorus, potassium, resolved sunlit/shaded leaf classes, acclimation and mortality thresholds are not represented.

Weather & uncertainty

These presets are synthetic, not observed weather from named locations. Daily solar geometry varies with date and latitude, including polar day/night. Clear-sky shortwave is estimated from solar elevation, Earth–Sun distance, and the selected transmission; PAR uses 45% of shortwave and 4.57 µmol J⁻¹. This is an approximation, not a measured irradiance product. Annual temperature follows a hemisphere-aware sinusoid. Rain probability follows a selectable single seasonal cycle. This does not reconstruct observed monsoons or bimodal rainy seasons. The variable-weather mode combines correlated daily temperature anomalies, cloud reduction, and Bernoulli rain days with exponential depths. Humidity responds to temperature and wet days. With variability off, mean daily rain arrives at dawn. The shaded biomass interval is the 10th–90th percentile across the weather repetitions, not a confidence interval or all model uncertainty.

Numerics & visualization

All pathways begin with the same structural mass, reserve fraction, allocation, root footprint and leaf-area conversion in matched mode. Plant-type mode intentionally changes these traits. States advance in ten-minute substeps and are recorded hourly. Each leaf unit has a persistent identity and accumulates allocated tissue until its target construction mass is reached. Maintenance losses and turnover reduce its surviving tissue. New units appear only when carbon and nitrogen are allocated. Hourly organ states interpolate continuously during playback. Attachment sites retain their organ identity. Stem thickness follows stem dry mass; leaf blade size follows surviving tissue and unfolding follows constructed tissue. Root extent is a schematic function of root mass, not predicted rooting depth. The root section uses its own fixed display scale. The 2D growth view interpolates recorded organ states; all visible tissue derives from resource allocations. Positions and geometry are illustrative. Broadleaf, cereal, rosette and pad architectures are illustrative visual mappings. Opuntia pads represent the generic photosynthetic-tissue pool: the simulator does not resolve succulent stem anatomy. Cereal reproductive cohorts are aggregated into two representative ears; sunflower cohorts into one representative head. Other forms show up to 18 reproductive units. These visualizations summarize the existing generic reproductive pools; species-specific flowering age, grain number and ripening are not predicted. Agave flowering in a short demo is an imposed allocation scenario, not a juvenile flowering prediction. Fixed camera framing allows growth to remain visible during playback. At most 80 photosynthetic units and 18 reproductive units are drawn per plant; totals always include all units. The 3D scene is not loaded. Received PAR sums irradiance over photosynthetic area without canopy shading; water and photon ratios per new dry mass are whole-plant operating ratios, not causal costs of individual organs. Carbon, water and nitrogen budget residuals are checked for every recorded state.

Weather data: Open-Meteo / ERA5 reanalysis (CC BY 4.0). Historical mode uses hourly temperature, RH, wind, rain (snow excluded), shortwave radiation and first-hour root-zone soil moisture. Missing data stops the run; no synthetic substitution. Historical repetitions are disabled. Radiation is a previous-hour mean; daily solar geometry remains calculated from latitude. Synthetic location settings are illustrative scenarios, not climate normals. Organ pools determine photosynthetic area and resource demand. Canopy shading and cell-resolved C4 energy exchange is not modeled. Scientific references: Hartzell et al. — Photo3 · von Caemmerer (2021) — C4 photosynthesis. These inform the model design; their validation does not validate this simplified implementation.

Calibration requires matching measurements to model outputs

Gas exchange alone cannot identify cyclic flow, proton coupling and respiratory demand separately. Combine A–Ci and light-response curves with fluorescence, P700 and preferably electrochromic-shift measurements; retain temperature, O₂, light spectrum, absorptance, cultivar and leaf age. For CAM, add a full diel CO₂ record, malate or acidity and reserve carbohydrates. For growth, measure leaf area, organ dry mass, SLA, nitrogen, irrigation and soil water through time. Reserve independent plants or conditions for validation.

Fluorescence ETR is an estimate dependent on absorptance and excitation partition. This lab displays potential electron-transport supply, so comparison requires an observation model. Its carbon uptake precedes maintenance and is not directly equivalent to measured net leaf gas exchange. Do not fit by simply renaming the columns.
